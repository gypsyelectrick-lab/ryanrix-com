## A Pamphlet and a Promise

In 1848, two men in their late twenties published a short pamphlet in London. It had been commissioned by a small political league, printed in a modest run, and written quickly. It carried no footnotes, no data, no budget tables, and no cost estimates. What it carried was a story - history as a sequence of class struggles, moving somewhere - and it ended with a program: ten numbered demands, plain enough for a movement to carry into a meeting and read aloud.

The Communist Manifesto is not a work of scholarship. It is a work of theater, and it is very good theater. It tells its readers that the world as it stands is a stage, that the present order is a passing one, and that they - the readers, the workers, the ones with nothing to lose but their chains - are the ones who will end it.

That is the form. Not a treatise, not a defense, not a plan for administrators. A manifesto is a document of the not-yet. It is written by people outside the gate, announcing a future that does not exist, addressed to a movement that is supposed to go and build it.

This article is not about whether Marx was right. It is about the shape of the thing he wrote, and about what happens when people try to write its mirror image.

## Four Books, One Title

The title "The Capitalist Manifesto" has been claimed four times that I can trace. Each claimant was serious. Each had a real argument. And each, in the end, wrote a different kind of book entirely.

The first came in 1958, from Random House. Louis O. Kelso was a lawyer and economist who would go on to invent the Employee Stock Ownership Plan, the ESOP - the structure that lets workers hold equity in the firms they work for. His co-author was Mortimer J. Adler, a neo-Thomist philosopher. Together they laid out three principles of economic justice: Participation, Distribution, Limitation. The book was a best-seller, and it became the theoretical basis of the ESOP. Its position is capital broadening - spreading ownership outward to workers, explicitly against concentration.

The second came in 2005, from University Press of America: *The Capitalist Manifesto: The Historic, Economic, and Philosophic Case for Laissez-Faire*, by Andrew Bernstein, who is affiliated with the Ayn Rand Institute. Its position is Randian, Objectivist, and absolute: markets unfettered.

The third came in 2021, from Robert Kiyosaki, the author of Rich Dad Poor Dad. His *Capitalist Manifesto* is a book about personal finance and investing - what an individual reader should do with their money.

The fourth came in 2023, from Johan Norberg: *The Capitalist Manifesto: Why the Global Free Market Will Save the World*. Its position is a defense of global free markets. Of Globalism, if you will.

Four books. Four serious authors. One title, worn by all of them.

## The Spread Is the First Clue

Set the first and second against each other and something strange appears.

Kelso wanted capital ownership spread to workers. Bernstein wanted markets unfettered. Those are not two shades of one position. They are close to opposites - one is a claim about who should hold the productive assets, the other is a claim about what should be allowed to happen to them. Put the two spines on a shelf beside each other and a reader who did not already know the arguments could not guess they shared a name.

This is not a scandal. It is a symptom. When a title keeps getting picked up by people who disagree with each other, the title is not doing the work a title is supposed to do. It is a label looking for a thing to name.

The word itself has a similar history. We have written before about <a href="/economics/the-name-is-the-first-argument/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">how the name of the system was coined by its critics</a> - a name nobody who supported it ever chose for themselves.

There is a smaller oddity worth a mention. Wikipedia's disambiguation page for the phrase lists only three of these books. It misses Bernstein entirely. The title has been claimed more often than a casual search will tell you.

## The Position Spread Is Not the Real Problem

It is tempting to conclude that the failure is marketing. Four books, one crowded name, nobody wins the shelf. That explanation is available, and it is wrong, or at least incomplete.

The real problem is structural. A manifesto is a form written by insurgents, and capitalism is the incumbent.

Read that again, because everything else follows from it. The Communist Manifesto is a pamphlet by men announcing an order that did not exist. It speaks from outside power to people who want in. Its whole rhetorical engine runs on the gap between the world as it is and the world as it should be, and on the promise that the latter is coming.

Now try to write that on behalf of the established order. The defender of the incumbent cannot say "the current arrangement must fall." He has to say the opposite: the current arrangement is good, under attack, and worth keeping. That is not a manifesto. That is a defense. The genres are different because the situations are different, and no amount of borrowed terminology can close the gap.

## Watch All Four Drift Out of the Form

Once you see the structural problem, the four books stop looking like four failed attempts and start looking like four different escape routes from a form that would not hold them.

Bernstein's subtitle is the philosophic case for laissez-faire. The word "case" is doing exactly what it sounds like. A case is argued. It is built from premises and evidence and addressed to a reader who has not yet agreed. That is a treatise.

Norberg's subtitle says the free market "will save the world." That is a defense - a promise of rescue for a system under indictment. A manifesto does not defend. It attacks.

Kiyosaki's book is a self-help book. It tells a reader what to do with a paycheck. Manifestos are not addressed to individuals optimizing their lives; they are addressed to a movement.

And Kelso and Adler, the most interesting of the four, wrote a policy argument aimed at legislators. Participation, Distribution, Limitation are not battle cries. They are design principles for a statute. The book that became the intellectual foundation of the ESOP was, from the first page, a document meant to be handed to people who write law.

None of the four is manifesto-shaped. A manifesto is short, narrative, and programmatic, and it is addressed to a movement. Four books, four escapes, one crowded title - and not one of them actually wearing the form.

## They Answer the Indictment, Not the Question

There is a second reason the form keeps slipping away, and it is subtler than the first.

All four books are defensive. Each one, in its own idiom, is making the same move: capitalism is good, actually. Kelso says broadened ownership is good. Bernstein says unfettered markets are good. Kiyosaki says participating in markets is good for you. Norberg says global markets are good for the world.

That is a rebuttal posture. Each book is answering a complaint - capitalism concentrates, capitalism is exploitative, capitalism is unstable, capitalism is cruel - and the answer is an argument that the complaint is wrong.

Here is the trouble. When you spend a book rebutting a complaint, the complaint has set your agenda. You are reacting. You are on the defensive in the plainest sense: you are explaining why the accusation does not stick.

A manifesto does not do that. A manifesto does not defend the present against a charge. It proposes a future against the present. It is a program, not a plea. It answers the question the indictment leaves open rather than the question the indictment asks.

And the question the indictment leaves open is this: what would capitalism have to become?

Not "is it good." Not "will it survive." Not "here is why the critics are wrong." What would it have to become - what would have to change, concretely, in who owns what and who decides what - for the system to deliver what its defenders claim and its critics deny?

That is the book the form is built for. And none of the four is it.

## What the Missing Book Would Look Like

Let us be fair to the form by describing what the absent book would actually be.

It would be short. Manifestos are read in a sitting and quoted for a century. Length is not a virtue in this genre; it is a drag on being carried and passed around.

It would be narrative. The Communist Manifesto works because it tells a story with a beginning, a middle, and an end that the reader is invited to help reach. A defense has no plot. A program does.

It would be programmatic. It would end in a numbered list - not of principles, but of changes. Not "participation" but a specific mechanism. Not "distribution" but a rule about who holds the title. The list would be short enough to memorize and concrete enough to argue about.

And it would be addressed to a movement. To people who are supposed to do something, not to legislators who might write something, and not to readers who might buy something.

What would such a program contain? That is a separate essay, and probably several. The point here is narrower. Whatever it contained, it would be a proposal about what capitalism should become, offered by people who want it to become that. It would not be a defense of what it already is.

That is a very different book from any of the four on the shelf.

## The Title Is Taken. The Form Is Open.

Step back and the pattern is clean.

The name has been claimed four times, by four serious people, holding positions that range from capital broadening to laissez-faire absolutism. Not one of them wrote a manifesto. Bernstein wrote a treatise. Norberg wrote a defense. Kiyosaki wrote a self-help book. Kelso and Adler wrote a policy argument for legislators. The spread of their positions is real, but the spread of their forms is the better clue: a title that attracts four incompatible books is a title that is not yet attached to a book.

The reason is not that these authors lacked talent or nerve. It is that they were trying to use a form built for the outsider while standing inside the gate. A manifesto announces a world that does not yet exist. Capitalism is not that world's absence. It is the world the reader already lives in. You cannot credibly announce what is already on the table.

So the conclusion is not that "The Capitalist Manifesto" is a bad title. It is that the title is taken and the form is open. The book the form is waiting for has not been written - because the book the form wants is not a defense, and defending is what everyone who reaches for the title ends up doing.

The next time a book arrives with that name on the cover, one question will tell you what it is. Flip to the end. Is there a program - a short list of what capitalism must become? Or is there an argument about whether it should have been allowed to exist?

Only one of those is a manifesto. Four books have worn the title. None has answered the question. The gap is not the name.
