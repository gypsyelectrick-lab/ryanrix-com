## The Natural Condition of Mankind

**Hobbes called it the natural condition of mankind - solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. He was describing the world the market escaped.**

In 1651, Thomas Hobbes published <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Leviathan</a>, and in the thirteenth chapter he set out to describe what life is like where no common power keeps order. He titled the chapter "Of the Natural Condition of Mankind, as concerning their Felicity, and Misery." His answer is the most quoted sentence in English political philosophy: the life of man is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

He was not being cruel. He was being accurate. For almost all of human history, that was the human condition. Most children died before adulthood. Most adults worked from dawn to dusk for enough food to see the next harvest. Famine, plague, and war were not interruptions to normal life. They *were* normal life.

And then something happened. Over two centuries, the world the market built changed the baseline of human existence. This series - The Natural Condition of Mankind - is the story of that change: where free market capitalism came from, what it replaced, and what it delivered.

But before the story can begin, we must deal with the name. Because the name is the first argument. Whoever names the thing gets to frame it.

## A Word in a Novel

The word capitalism entered English in 1854, and the first man to write it down was not an economist. He was a novelist, William Makepeace Thackeray, and he put the word in his novel <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Newcomes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Newcomes</a> - a long, warm, sprawling story about money and marriage in respectable Victorian London.

The word meant nothing more than "having ownership of capital." That is the entire meaning: the condition of owning capital. No system. No ideology. No indictment. A novelist needed a word for the plain fact of owning things that produce income, and he reached for "capitalism."

Nobody reading The Newcomes in 1854 thought they were reading the name of a system. Because nobody thought there was a system to name. There was just what people did: trade, lend, build, hire, sell, save. It was as natural as weather, and as little in need of a label.

The word stayed innocent for eighteen years.

## The Sneer Arrives

In 1872, the same word changed its meaning. The Oxford English Dictionary records it: from 1872, "capitalism" meant a political and economic system - and, as <a href="https://www.etymonline.com/word/capitalism" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">etymonline</a> puts it, it was "originally used disparagingly by socialists." The <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">history of the word capitalism</a> is on the record, and that is where the sneer enters it.

The French had gotten there first. Louis Blanc used "capitalisme" in 1850, in a pamphlet about the organization of labor, and Proudhon used it in 1861. The word was born in French as an accusation and crossed the Channel wearing the same expression.

Between 1854 and 1872, nothing had changed in the thing itself. The system was not new in 1872 - it had been growing for centuries. What was new was the name as a verdict. The word stopped being a description and became a charge. Say "capitalism" and you had already said "guilty".

Nobody who ran a mill, captained a ship, or kept a ledger had asked for this name. The people who did the thing never named the thing. The people who objected to it did. That is the pattern of the next century and a half, and this article is the record of it.

## The Critic Who Named the Villain

Now we come to the most important critic, and we should be generous with him, because generosity is owed. Karl Marx's critique was real, and it was built on real suffering. Friedrich Engels walked the streets of Manchester in the 1840s and documented what the mills did to the people who worked in them, in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Condition_of_the_Working_Class_in_England" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Condition of the Working Class in England</a>. The book is a record of genuine horror: children working sixteen hours, lungs destroyed by cotton dust, families packed into cellars. Anyone who reads it and shrugs has not really read it.

The critique was real. The prescription was not, and the difference between the two is the subject of this series. But first, notice what Marx did with the word.

Marx did not coin "capitalism." He inherited it from the system's critics, who had been using it disparagingly for nearly two decades - Louis Blanc in France in 1850, Proudhon in 1861 - and he made it the villain of the most famous book in economics. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Kapital" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Das Kapital</a>, published in 1867, is the indictment of the system, and in its pages Marx mostly wrote of the "capitalist mode of production." The word itself he left largely alone.

That is the thing to notice. The critics named the thing, and the name carried the indictment inside it. "Capitalism" entered the language as a neutral noun and left as a charge sheet. Say the word today and you have already said the verdict. The argument is over before the evidence is presented.

## The Seminar Adopts It

The socialists made the word an insult. The academy made it respectable. In 1902, the German economist Werner Sombart published <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Sombart" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Der moderne Kapitalismus</a> - "Modern Capitalism" - and the word became a scholarly category, fit for seminars and monographs.

Now the professors used the socialists' label. The word shed its sneer and gained a bibliography. A term of abuse became a term of art.

And still, nobody who actually did the thing called himself a capitalist. That identity arrived only in the twentieth century, and mostly as a shrug - a label accepted from outside, not chosen from within. The builders of the system never named it. They were too busy building.

## The Man Who Named the Big Bang

The parallel is exact, and it deserves the full story, because it is one of the great accidents of intellectual history.

The year was 1949. The place was a BBC studio, and the man at the microphone was <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Fred Hoyle</a>, a Cambridge astronomer with a combative streak and a theory he loved. The theory was the steady state: the universe had no beginning and no end. It had always existed, and it always would. Matter was created continuously, a few atoms a year in a volume the size of a living room, just enough to keep the whole thing looking the same forever.

The rival theory held that everything had started at once. All matter, all energy, all space and time, compressed into a single point, released in a single moment. Hoyle thought the idea was childish, and he wanted a phrase that would make it sound as childish as he believed it was.

He sat at the microphone, on 28 March 1949, and he gave the rival theory its name: "one big bang at a particular time in the remote past."

The phrase was meant to bury the theory. Instead it named it.

Today every cosmology textbook in the world uses Hoyle's dismissive phrase. The Big Bang is the theory's name, used with perfect seriousness by the people who believe it. Hoyle spent the rest of his career fighting the theory he had named. He would later insist he had not meant the phrase derisively; the safe claim is the strong one, and it is enough. The name was coined by the theory's most prominent opponent. Years later he told an interviewer, as <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00894-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Nature</a> recounted on the phrase's seventy-fifth anniversary, that "words are like harpoons. Once they go in, they are very hard to pull out."

And the man whose observations forced the theory on an unwilling profession? Edwin Hubble never called it anything but the redshift-distance relation. He observed the expansion. He never named it. The rival named it.

The man who named the Big Bang did not believe in it. The name came from the enemy. It frequently does.

## The Man Who Never Used the Word

Now the pivot. In 1776, Adam Smith published The Wealth of Nations, and in it he described the entire machinery of commercial society: the division of labor, the pin factory, the market as a conversation between strangers, the way self-interest is channeled into general prosperity. It is the founding description of the system this series is about.

Smith never used the word "capitalism." The word did not exist when he wrote. He could not have used it. But that is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is what Smith thought he was doing. He thought he was describing what happens naturally. Exchange, specialization, improvement - things people do when they are left alone. He describes; he does not prescribe. That is the dividing line of this whole series.

The market's defenders describe what people do. Its enemies prescribe what people must do. Smith described. Marx prescribed. Lenin prescribed. Mao prescribed. Mussolini prescribed. Hitler prescribed. Every one of them had a design and an apparatus to impose it. Smith had a pen and a window.

The system Smith described rests on foundations we have examined before: the <a href="/economics/property-rights-the-foundation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">property rights</a> that make exchange possible, and the plain fact that <a href="/economics/the-free-market-is-more-democratic/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">free markets are more democratic than voting</a> - every purchase a ballot, cast continuously, by everyone. The prescribers' systems all required apparatuses. Smith's system required nothing but freedom.

## The Renaming Machine

And the critics have been renaming the thing ever since, once a generation, like a machine that stamps out a new label every few decades.

"Capitalism" was the name of 1872, the socialist sneer. "Neoliberalism" was next. The word was actually self-adopted at the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloque_Walter_Lippmann" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Colloque Walter Lippmann</a> in 1938, proposed by Alexander Rüstow as a name for a renewed classical liberalism. The critics captured it anyway: by the 1990s, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"neoliberalism"</a> had become a left-wing epithet, a synonym for everything wrong with the world, used mostly by people who could not define it. Same word game, new label.

"Late-stage capitalism" is the current model. It is Marxist in origin - Ernest Mandel's book of 1972 - and it was popularized by Mark Fisher's <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalist_Realism" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Capitalist Realism</a> in 2009. The name is an obituary. It says the system is dying, the end is near, history has already convicted it.

Three names. Three indictments. All from critics. And the newest name is an obituary - which is the one thing the critics have been getting wrong since 1811, when the Luddites smashed the machines they were sure would end their world. They were defeated and the world did not end. The GDP-zero chain is the newest obituary for the same system, and we dismantled it in <a href="/economics/the-luddites-were-half-right/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Luddites Were Half Right</a>. The obituary has been wrong for two centuries. The renaming machine keeps stamping out fresh ones anyway.

## The Lens

The critics always get to name the thing. That is the pattern of a century and a half, and it is not an accident: whoever names the thing gets to frame it. Say "capitalism" and you have already said the verdict. Say "neoliberalism" and you have already said the verdict. Say "late-stage capitalism" and you have written the obituary.

The question is whether you adopt their frame. The naming contest is one front in the wider conflict that Max Weber mapped in <a href="/politics/battle-of-ideas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Battle of Ideas</a>. The first move in that battle is always the name.

In this series we are going to look at what the name actually describes - where it came from, what it replaced, and what it delivered. Hobbes called it the natural condition of mankind - solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. He was describing the world the market escaped. The rest of this series is the story of the escape.
