## The Promise

Three articles ago, we ended on the same question. In <a href="/politics/the-fabian-grip/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Fabian Grip</a>, we watched a 142-year-old society place its people into every institution that shapes British policy - and count every Labour Prime Minister among its members. In <a href="/politics/wrong-about-everything/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wrong About Everything</a>, we asked whether the Conservative Party ever had an intellectual foundation worth auditing. In <a href="/politics/the-red-green-alliance/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Red-Green Alliance</a>, we asked whether it ever had an institution behind it at all.

The answer to all three is the same: no.

The Conservative Party is the institution that wasn't. It never built the Fabian equivalent - a machine that generates ideas, incubates leaders, and enforces orthodoxy across decades. It has fragments: the Carlton Club, the Bow Group, the Conservative Research Department, the network of Tufton Street think tanks. But those are competing pipelines that come and go. None of them is continuous. None of them grips the party the way the Fabian Society grips Labour.

That absence is not a detail. It explains the party's defining behavior - the one we have watched all summer.

---

## What the Fabians Actually Built

To see what is missing, look at what the Fabians have.

A continuous institution does four things. It incubates leaders - every Labour PM was a Fabian. It generates ideas - the welfare state was built from Fabian tracts. It sets intellectual boundaries - the party argues within them, not about them. And it enforces orthodoxy without being the party itself, so the machine outlasts every leader, every faction, every election cycle.

The Conservative Party has never had that. It has had moments. The Bow Group mattered in the 1950s. The Conservative Research Department mattered under Butler and Thatcher. Tufton Street has mattered in bursts since the 2000s. But each was a project of its moment, dependent on its patron, and dissolved when the wind changed.

The result: the party's orthodoxy is not set by any institution. It is set by whoever is leading at the time - and it swings.

---

## The Exhibit: Six Years Ago

The net zero flip is the cleanest demonstration of the difference.

In 2019, the Conservative Party legislated net zero. It was the first major economy in the world to do it. The law (an amendment to Labour's Climate Change Act of 2008) was passed by a Conservative government, with Conservative votes, on Conservative policy - and it was celebrated in the party's own terms as a global leadership moment.

This month, the party's current leader purged the candidates who still believe it.

That is not a policy change. Policy changes happen in every party, and they are usually argued out in the institutions before they land. This was not argued out. The candidates were filtered - removed from the list because they did not meet the leader's red lines on the very policy the party legislated six years ago.

A party with an institution would have had the argument in the machine first. The institution would have generated the new position, built the case, softened the landing, mostly out of the public eye. The Conservative Party does not have that machine. So the leader's red lines become the party's position by fiat - and the people who disagree are removed, not persuaded.

---

## The Filter: What It Actually Selects For

Here is the mechanism made visible, in four acts.

**Act one: the filter.** The purge is described as rooting out candidates who do not believe in the leader's agenda. In practice, the filter tests loyalty to the leader's red lines, not adherence to any doctrine. The net zero moderates go. That is the purge's own accounting: the 2019 law, once Conservative orthodoxy, is now a disqualifier.

**Act two: the admission.** The same filter let a man through who was jailed for antisemitic harassment. He was then given the party's anti-extremism adviser role. When the story broke, he withdrew - but the members backed the decision to have him there in the first place.

**Act three: the members.** The party's own membership survey confirmed the backing. The base does not see a problem with the filter admitting a convicted harasser, because the filter was never testing for character or doctrine. It was testing for loyalty.

**Act four: the vacuum.** With no institution to generate ideas, policy becomes whatever the news cycle demands. In one week: an emergency law against early release - the Opposition's answer to the prison release row. A headline attack on the Prime Minister over North Sea drilling. Both announced as if they were policy. Both are positioning.

That is what fills the vacuum when there is no machine. Not ideas. Announcements.

---

## The Review That Says It All

The party's own policy review - the one modeled on Keith Joseph's 1970s intellectual renewal - has already declared the 2019 net zero law "impossible."

I was surprised to discover that. A review set up to rebuild the party's intellectual foundations concluded that the party's flagship law is impossible. And the response to that conclusion was not an argument. It was a purge of the people who might disagree.

The review is the institution that wasn't. It exists to generate ideas, and the party's reaction to its conclusion is to remove the people who could debate it. An institution would have used the review to sharpen the argument. The Conservative Party used it to clean the list.

---

## What This Explains

Every pattern we have watched this summer - the fragmentation, the purges, the policy-by-announcement, the leader's grip tightening while the party's intellectual base hollows out - is the same absence expressing itself.

The Fabians spent 142 years building a machine that shaped every Labour government. The Conservative Party never built the equivalent. It has the Carlton Club and the Bow Group and Tufton Street, but no continuous institution - nothing that outlasts the leader, nothing that generates ideas before the news cycle demands them, nothing that can tell a leader "no."

So the leader filters instead. The filter removes the moderates, admits the loyalists, and produces policy by announcement. And then the party wonders why it keeps fragmenting.

The answer was in the question we asked three articles ago. It has never had an institution behind it at all.

---

## The Closing Question

The Fabians spent 142 years building a machine that shaped every Labour government. The current Conservative leader is filtering candidates instead - and the filter admitted a man jailed for antisemitic harassment while blocking moderates who believe in net zero, made him the party's adviser on extremism, watched the members back it, and then answered the week's biggest stories with a borrowed law and a headline attack.

What exactly is the party selecting for - and what does that tell you about why the Conservatives keep fragmenting?
