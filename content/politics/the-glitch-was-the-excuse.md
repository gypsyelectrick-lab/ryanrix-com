New Jersey's Motor Vehicle Commission asks every license applicant, almost as an afterthought, whether they want to be added to the voter rolls. It is presented as a convenience: one form, one counter, democracy included. Thousands of people clicked yes. A smaller number clicked no, or never answered. Around 6,600 people who should not have been on the rolls were registered anyway.

Here is the detail that makes the story: many of those applicants told the state, in the same transaction, that they were not United States citizens. The form asked. They answered honestly. The system registered them as voters anyway. Around 400 of them voted before the error came to light.

The word everyone reached for was "glitch." This is the latest episode in the <a href="/politics/why-things-break-introduction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Why Things Break</a> series, and the word is the subject. The glitch is not the story. The glitch is the excuse.

## The applicants did everything right

New Jersey's registration error is often reported as a story about bad software. It is a story about something more interesting: a task that was assigned to nobody.

The application asks about citizenship because eligibility is the law's requirement. Federal law forbids noncitizens from voting in federal elections. States are required to maintain accurate rolls. The state's own form collects the information needed to make the judgment. Someone still had to look at the answer and act on it. In New Jersey, in the system at issue, nobody did.

What does a "glitch" excuse accomplish? It moves the failure out of the human world and into the machine. A machine cannot be held responsible, so nobody is. This is the same move the state makes whenever the machinery disappoints it: blame the technology, hire a consultant, move on. The people who design the process never appear in the story, which is why the process never changes. The pattern is documented in <a href="/economics/dont-call-them-technocrats/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Don't Call Them Technocrats</a>: the technocrat framing lets the structure escape while the individuals inside it collect their salaries.

The applicants did everything right. The system did nothing it claimed.

## The mechanism: nobody owns the check

Voter registration in practice is a pipeline. Applications flow in, get processed, and names land on rolls. The system is measured by throughput: how many applications processed, how fast, how many registrations delivered. It is not measured by how many ineligible people were caught, because catching them costs throughput.

The people who ran the motor vehicle system had every incentive to process and none to verify. Their job was volume. Eligibility belongs to the election officials. But the election officials never see the application, because the motor vehicle system registers directly. Two agencies, one shared duty, zero owners. The eligibility check lived in the seam between them, and seams are where nobody works.

The same absence of ownership produced the <a href="/politics/why-things-break-nhs-it/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ten billion pound nothing</a> and <a href="/politics/why-things-break-birmingham-oracle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Birmingham's £225 million Oracle</a>: grand systems, announced confidently, with nobody in the chain responsible for whether they actually did the thing they were meant for. A health record system with no one accountable for the records. An accounting system with no one accountable for the accounts. A voter registration system with no one accountable for who gets registered. Different logos, identical seam.

The stakes are not theoretical. Local elections in New Jersey have been decided by a handful of votes. In one reported case, the margin was a single vote. Dismissals like "it is 0.001 percent of registrations" miss the point entirely: when a glitch can flip a seat, the word "glitch" is doing a lot of work.

## The exhibits up the chain

The failure alone would be a scandal. What followed is the demonstration. Every level of government has responded with motion, not ownership.

**The state refuses the records.** On July 28, New Jersey declined a Justice Department request for the registration records, per <a href="https://www.politico.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">POLITICO</a>. The records themselves are now contested territory. The federal government wants to measure the failure; the state will not hand over the tape. When the facts of a failure become a jurisdictional dispute, the dispute is the story: nobody is disputing who owns the check, because nobody owns it.

**The federal case is dismissed.** Around July 29, the federal case over the registrations was dismissed. Nobody adjudicated the substance. A case exists to decide a question; this one ended without the question being answered, and that was treated as normal.

**The President inflates the numbers.** On August 5, <a href="https://www.factcheck.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">FactCheck.org</a> noted that the President's claim of 35,000 noncitizens registered was an overstatement. The best available count is about 6,600. The gap is not sloppiness. It is the point. Nobody knows the true size of the failure, because measuring it was also nobody's job. When nobody is paid to count, the number becomes a political weapon, and both sides get to fire it.

**Congress writes to the vendor.** The House Judiciary and House Administration committees wrote to IDEMIA, the company behind the system, asking how a stated noncitizen became a registered voter. Even the basic question, whether this was a software error at all, is now a federal dispute, per the committees' own <a href="https://judiciary.house.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">statements</a> and <a href="https://cha.house.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">release</a>. The vendor gets the letter. The structure gets nothing. The contractor is accountable for the code. Nobody is accountable for the check. This is the old fight, the one <a href="/politics/battle-of-ideas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Battle of Ideas</a> describes: the question is never whether power worked, only who controls the account of it.

**The deadlines land.** The two committees' records demand to New Jersey's Secretary of State, dated August 3, asked for the documents by 5 p.m. on August 17. Representative Van Drew and 17 Republicans sent Governor Sherrill twelve questions, due August 21. As of the writing of this article, the state had not responded to either.

Notice what every exhibit has in common. The state says the records are secret. The courts say the case is dismissed. The President says the number is different. Congress says the vendor must explain. The Governor says nothing yet. Every institution is doing something. None of them is doing the check. The flurry is the point: a system with no owner responds to failure with paperwork about the failure. When the state's own electric vehicle mandate fell short, the answer was a consultation about the target, not a change of course, as documented in <a href="/politics/the-consultation-was-the-retreat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Consultation Was the Retreat</a>. When the rolls fail, the answer is letters about the rolls. Same pattern: motion in place of ownership.

## The closing exhibit

While the state counts the cost, the mechanism that produced the error is being demanded again. Student groups are campaigning to make voter registration easier still, per <a href="https://www.politicshome.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PoliticsHome</a>. Frictionless registration was the point of the motor vehicle integration, and frictionless is exactly what produced 6,600 registrations with no check behind them. The campaign is not asking for a better check. It is asking for more of the same pipeline, faster. The lesson drawn from the failure is that there was not enough of the failure's cause.

Meanwhile, the human cost is landing on the people who did nothing wrong. The <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">New York Times</a> reports that the improperly registered could lose their green-card path. The applicants who told the truth on the form now face consequences for the state's failure to read the form. The people who did everything right are the ones paying.

That is the grievance machine's signature, and it is described fully in <a href="/politics/the-grievance-machine-part-ii/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Grievance Machine, Part II: Who Profits from Panic</a>. The machine does not need the check to be fixed. It needs the panic to continue. Every actor in this story, the state protecting its records, the President inflating the number, the committees sending letters, the activists demanding more frictionless registration, benefits from the failure staying unresolved. None benefits from the check finally becoming someone's job.

## The lens

A system that registers the people who explicitly say they are not eligible does not have a glitch. It has an owner who was never given the job of checking.

Eligibility was not a technical problem. It was an assignment problem. The application collected the answer. The law defined the standard. The machinery moved the paper. Nobody was paid to close the loop. That is not a bug in the software. It is a decision about who counts, made by nobody, in the seam between two agencies.

New Jersey will purge the rolls. The state will argue with the federal government about the records. The committees will get their answers or they will escalate. The students will keep campaigning. And the next integration, the next convenience, the next pipeline will be built with the same missing seam, unless someone is paid to be right.

If nobody is paid to be right, what exactly is the government paying for?
